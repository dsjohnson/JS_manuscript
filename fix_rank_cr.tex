%% ABSTRACT %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

{\bf Abstract}. Bayesian estimation of abundance with capture-recapture data has been dominated for nearly 20 years by the parameter-expansion data-augmentation (PX-DA) approach. The PX-DA approach expands the parameter set to include the latent true states of the individuals. PX-DA allows straightforward coding of models in MCMC software such as {\tt JAGS} or {\tt nimble}, however, this approach can be computationally demanding for large or low detectability populations. We develop a 2-stage collapsed Gibbs sampler to reduce computational burden when using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to fit Jolly-Seber (JS) models. Using the collapsed sampler, no parameter expansion or data augmentation is needed. Two of the main computational benefits are: (1) the MCMC only needs to iterate over unique capture-histories and (2) the second stage of the sampler can be run in parallel. We provide an {\tt R} package {\tt nimbleJSextras} that uses the functionality of the {\tt nimble} and {\tt nimbleEcology} packages to perform MCMC analysis for JS models with the same ease for practitioners the PX-DA approach.  Using the {\tt nimbleJSextras} package, we analyzed two real data sets to illustrate the method. First we analyzed the classic dipper data using {\it per capita} recruitment parameterization. In the second example we analyzed a data set of 5,271 female nesting green turtles in the Northwest Hawaiian Islands over a 44 year period that contained time by individual behavioral effects induced by capture procedures. In the dipper analysis the relative speed of the MCMC compared to the PX-DA sampler depended on the number of augmented individuals, slower for 300 augmented individuals and faster for 1,000 individuals. Convergence metrics showed slightly quicker convergence of the collapsed MCMC than the PX-DA sampler. The full 2-stage collapsed sampler ran in approximately 1/10 the amount of time needed for the PX-DA sampler. The more complex green turtle data finished in $<3.5$ hours illustrating that relatively large, long-term data can be analyzed efficiently even with a complex capture probability model using the 2-stage collapsed sampler. Moreover, trial analyses are not necessary to determine an adequate number of augmented individuals.

\section{Introduction}

For over 60 years, open-population capture–recapture models, particularly those in the Jolly–Seber (JS) family \citep{Jolly1965,Seber1965}, have been fundamental tools for estimating demographic rates such as survival, recruitment, and population size in open systems where individuals can enter or leave the population over the course of study. The JS models estimate the parameters of these processes through repeated sampling of the population on discrete occasions and recording captures of uniquely identifiable individuals. Classical maximum-likelihood estimators and their extensions \citep{schwarz1996general} have long provided practical means for inference, yet they are often limited in their capacity to accommodate hierarchical structures, covariate effects, or complex dependence among parameters. Bayesian approaches have addressed many of these limitations, enabling flexible modeling and coherent quantification of uncertainty \citep{Royle:2008kx}. 

Adoption of Bayesian inference for JS models was initially hindered in regular use because standard Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) software at the time, such as {\tt WinBUGS} \citep{LunnEtAl2000a} or {\tt JAGS} \citep{plummer2003jags} did not have the capability to calculate the JS likelihood within the MCMC sampler. Therefore, practitioners would have to code the JS likelihood as well as bespoke MCMC algorithms for Bayesian estimation of JS parameters. The groundbreaking papers of \cite{Durban:2005yw} and \cite{Royle:2007kx} introduced the concept of a population of augmented individuals. This parameter expansion data augmentation (PX-DA) specification was formalized for JS models by \cite{RoyleDorazio2012a}. By introducing latent inclusion indicators as part of the hierarchical model, PX-DA allows MCMC sampling in a fixed-dimensional parameter space, where abundance is simply the sum of the latent inclusion indicators and not a model parameter. This breakthrough allowed users to construct a JS model hierarchically in {\tt WinBUGS} or {\tt JAGS} using simple, standard distributions.

Although data augmentation was instrumental in ushering in a new era of Bayesian JS modeling, the convenience comes at a cost. The approach can be computationally inefficient for analyses requiring a large amount of augmentation \citep{Royle:2007kx} and is prone to slow mixing \citep{SchofieldBarker2008a}. To alleviate some of these issues and speed computation \cite{yackulic2020need} and \cite{saracco2023fast} proposed the ``marginalized'' capture-recapture models, which formulates capture-recapture models as a Hidden Markov Model (HMM; \citealt{zucchini2016hidden}). The HMM forward algorithm allows one to marginalize over latent states so that they need not be sampled as part of the MCMC, however, the user still needs to augment data for undetected individuals if abundance estimation is desired. In this paper, we introduce a fully marginalized Bayesian formulation of the JS model that eliminates the need for any state or data augmentation. Our approach integrates analytically over both the unobserved individual encounter histories and the latent states of all individuals using a conditional HMM formulation of JS family models. 

The fully marginalized JS model is motivated by ``collapsing'' the PX-DA Gibbs sampler. A collapsed Gibbs sampler is a method to improve MCMC convergence over standard Gibbs samplers. \cite{Liu1994a} provides a mathematical proof which shows that the rate of convergence for a collapsed Gibbs sampler is at least as good, but often better, than the standard Gibbs sampler for any particular model. Unfortunately, we cannot directly use the theoretical results to prove that the collapsed Gibbs sampler for JS models will always converge at a faster rate because neither the PX-DA sampler nor the collapsed version developed here use strictly Gibbs updates. That is, at least some, if not all, of the parameters are updated using Metropolis-within-Gibbs updates because the full conditional distributions are not standard. However, studies have shown that MCMC convergence for collapsed samplers using Metropolis updates can still offer improvement but it may not be as good as strictly Gibbs updates \citep{VanDykJiao2015a}.  

The overall goal of this paper is to develop and demonstrate the collapsed approach for JS models using the MCMC software in the {\tt R} package {\tt nimble} \citep{Valpine:2017vg} to illustrate that PX-DA methods are not necessary and JS models can be fit without data augmentation in regular practice. The {\tt nimble} package allows users to create their own distribution functions which can be compiled and included within MCMC model statements. Using this capability, as well as the HMM distributions within the {\tt R} package {\tt nimbleEcology} \citep{GoldsteinEtAl2024a} we developed an {\tt R} package, {\tt nimbleJSextras}\footnote{Available at \url{https://github.com/dsjohnson/nimbleJSextras}} that includes the JS distribution and other functions for performing MCMC analysis of JS models with the collapsed Gibbs sampler. The benefits of this approach include the fact it is not necessary to specify a fixed number of augmented individuals that were not captured. Thus, practitioners do not need to worry about possible biases induced by adding too few augmented individuals \citep{link2013cautionary} or the additional time to assess what amount of augmentation is necessary to avoid this bias. Moreover, because augmented individuals are not used, the likelihood scales with the number of unique capture-histories, making it more efficient for large samples and populations. Finally, we can use the likelihood factoring approach of \cite{king2015capture} and \cite{Hooten:2023aa} to split the sampler into two stages where the second stage abundance sample can be executed in parallel. We demonstrate its use and benefits through analysis the classic European dipper ({\it Cinclus cinclus}) data as well as more complex breeding return-time model for Hawaiian green sea turtles (honu; {\it Chelonia mydas}). The analyses illustrate that augmentation is not necessary and the fully marginal collapsed Gibbs sampling approach can be used by practitioners just as easily as PX-DA methods. 


\section{The Jolly-Seber class of models}

Modern (POPAN) JS models have two population concepts, the population and the active population \citep{crosbie1985parsimonious,schwarz1996general}. The population is the collection of $N$ individuals that are available for capture in at least one sampling occasion during the study period. The occasion specific active population is composed of $N_t$ individuals that are (potentially) available for capture on sampling occasion $t=1,\dots,K$. Individuals enter, transition, and exit the active population through a Markov process where an individual can be in one of $J+2$ states, $0,\dots,J+1$ where $s=0$ is the pre-entry (pre-birth) state and $s = J+1$ is the post-exit (death) absorbing state where the individual is inactive for the remainder of the study. The intermediate states,  $s = 1,\dots,J$, are states where the animal is potentially available for capture. These may be biological or demographic in nature such as breeding or temporary emigration. The notation $z_{i,t} \in \{0,\dots,J+1\}$ will represent the true state of individual $i$ on occasion $t$, thus $N_t = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathcal{I}(1 \le z_{i,t} \le J)$, where $\mathcal{I}(\cdot)$ is and indicator function. 

Entry into the active population (e.g., birth) is governed by the multinomial probabilities, $\xi_t$, $t=1,\dots,K$ ($\sum_{t=1}^K\xi_t = 1$),  which are the unconditional probabilities that an individual enters between occasions $t-1$ and $t$ and is available for capture on occasion $t$  \citep{crosbie1985parsimonious,schwarz1996general}. When an individual first enters the active population between $t-1$ and $t$, the probability that it enters into state $s$ is $\alpha_{s,t}$, where $\sum_{s=1}^{J} \alpha_{s,t}=1$. Given that an individual has entered prior to occasion $t$, the probability that an individual remains in the active population between occasions $t$ and $t+1$ (e.g., survives), given it is currently in state $s$, is $\phi_{s,t}$. Finally, $\psi_{r,s,t}$ ($\sum_{s=1}^{J}\psi_{r,s,t} = 1$) is the probability that an individual transitions from states $r$ to $s$ between times $t$ and $t+1$ given it was in the active population at time $t$ and survived to occasion $t+1$. 

Data for the JS model are composed of capture histories for each individual captured at least once, $\bx_i = (x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,K})$ where $x_{i,t} \in \{0,1,\dots,O\}$ is recorded as 0 if the individual is not captured or 1 of $O$ observable states when it is captured. We use the notation $[x_{i,t}|s]$ to represent the likelihood of observing $x_{i,t}$ on occasion $t$ given the individual is in state $z_{it} = s$. For a traditional JS model where the observation is binary (i.e., 0 = not captured, 1 = captured), 
\[
[x_{i,t}|s] = \left\{
\begin{array}{rll}
& (1-p_{s,t})(1-x_{i,t}) + p_{s,t}x_{i,t},&s=1,\dots,J\\
& 1-x_{i,t},\text{ for }& s=0 \text{ or } J+1
\end{array} \right.
\]      
and $p_{s,t}$ is the probability of capture in state $s$ on occasion $t$.

To provide a framework for developing the collapsed Gibbs sampler, we provide a brief review of the PX-DA approach for Bayesian JS inference. Let $\bx$ represent the $n\times K$ capture history matrix, $\bz$ is the associated matrix of true state values. $\bx_{aug} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\bz_{aug}$ are the $M-n \times K$ matrices for the $M-n$ augmented individuals. For the PX-DA approach one combines, $\bx^+ = (\bx',\bx_{aug}')'$ and $\bz^+ = (\bz',\bz_{aug}')'$, and treats them as observed data and true states respectively. The indicator of population inclusion is $\bw^+ = (\mathbf{1},\bw_{aug}')'$. The posterior distribution for inference is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pxda.post}
[\bt,\gamma, \bw_{aug}, \bz^+|\bx^+] \propto [\bx^+|\bz^+,\bw^+,\bt][\bz^+|\bt][\bw^+|\gamma][\bt][\gamma],
\end{equation}
where $\bt$ are the parameters of the JS model described in the previous section and $\gamma$ is the probability that an individual is a member of the population. The main benefit of the PX-DA approach is that all the distributions on the right-hand side of (\ref{eq:pxda.post}) are standard distributions, so they can readily be described in the model statements for MCMC software such as {\tt JAGS} and {\tt nimble}. 

%and the dimension of the products remain fixed at $M$ individuals so trans-dimensional methods are not needed. Gibbs sampling for the PX-DA proceeds by sequentially sampling from the conditional distributions: $[\bz^+|\bt,\bw^+,\bx^+]$, $[\bw^+|\bt,\gamma,\bz^+,\bx^+]$,
%$[\gamma|\bw^+]$, and $[\bt|\bz^+,\bw^+,\bx^+]$. Of course, these may be broken up further into scalar parameter updates. During updates, the raw quantities $\bz^+$ and $\bw^+$, as well as, $\gamma$ are not usually useful on their 
%own and abundance metric summaries, say $\bvt=f(\bz^+,\bw^+)$, are usually stored such as $N=\bw'\mathbf{1}$ or $N_t=\sum_{i=1}^M z_{it}w_i$. So, the resulting posterior desired is usually, $[\bt,\bvt|\bx^+]$. Note, that the posterior distribution used for inference depends on the size of the augmentation data set, so it is not the exact posterior, however, if $M$ is chosen large enough it is a very good approximation. 

\section{Collapsed Gibbs sampling for Jolly-Seber models}

Collapsed Gibbs samplers are constructed by marginalizing over some components of the model, using a standard Gibbs sampler to sample the remaining parameters, then the marginalized parameters are sampled using their posterior predictive distribution given the first sample. For example, to sample from the posterior $[\bt_1,\bt_2|\bx]$, the corresponding collapsed sampler would first draw from $[\bt_1|\bx]\propto [\bx|\bt_1][\bt_1]$ then draw from the posterior predictive distribution $[\bt_2|\bt_1,\bx]$. Relative to a standard Gibbs sampler, faster convergence is achieved because the first sample is not conditioned on the second sample components, reducing the covariance between them \citep{Liu1994a}. In the case of JS models, $\bt_1 = (\bt, \gamma)$ and $\bt_2 = (\bz^+, \bw_{aug})$.

Therefore one usually saves the same fixed-dimensional abundance metrics as in the PX-DA scenario, $\bvt = f(\bz,\bz_u,n_u)$. Note the function, $f$ is slightly different than before. \subsection{Sampling from the marginal posterior of Jolly-Seber models}

In the first step we need to draw a sample from the marginal posterior distribution, $[\bt,\gamma|\bx]$, so let us start with the PX-DA posterior and marginalize over the augmented components. To accomplish this we extend the approach used by \cite{king2015capture} and \cite{Hooten:2023aa} for closed-population models to open models by partitioning the PX-DA posterior by conditioning on whether individuals are captured then integrating over the augmentation components to obtain,
\[
\relax[\bt,\gamma, \bz|\bx] \propto [\bx,\bz|\bt,\bc] [n|\bt,\gamma][\bt][\gamma]
\]
where, $[\bx,\bz|\bt,\bc]$ is the conditional distribution of the capture histories and states given the individuals where captured at least once, indicated by ``$\bc$,'' and $[n|\bt,\gamma] = \text{Binomial}(M, \gamma p^*)$ is the probability of capturing $n$ individuals over the course of study, where $p^*$ is the probability of being captured at least once. 
%Note that the left-hand side of $[\bt,\gamma, \bz|\bx]$ does not condition on $\bc$ this is because $\bc$ is a deterministic function of $\bx$. So, after $\bx$ is accounted for, $\bc$ is redundant. 

%Before we continue with marginalizing over $\bz$ let us take a closer look at  $[n|\bt,\gamma]$. 
We do not have any augmented data in the marginal posterior, but we still have the remnants of that process, $M$ and $\gamma$. We remove their effect by allowing $M\to \infty$. If we follow this limit while simultaneously allowing $\gamma \to 0$ such that $M\gamma \to \lambda$, then  $[n|\bt,\gamma] \to [n|\bt,\lambda] = \text{Poisson}(\lambda p^*)$.  So, marginalizing over the components of an infinitely large augmented data set, yields the posterior,
\[
[\bt,\lambda, \bz|\bx] \propto  [\bx,\bz|\bt,\bc][n|\bt, \lambda][\bt][\lambda].
\]
%This is equivalent in form to the likelihood derived by \cite{glennie2019open} and \cite{zhang2023flexible} for spatial capture-recapture models when individuals are distributed as a spatial Poisson process.

Now the next step in evaluating the fully marginal posterior distribution, is to marginalize over the (partially) latent states, $\bz$. \cite{mcclintock2020uncovering} shows an \emph{unconditional} individual capture history in a JS models can be formulated as a Hidden Markov Model (HMM),
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq.uncond.lik}
[\bx_i|\bt] = \bpi\bP_1(x_{i1})\bG_1\bP_2(x_{i2}) \cdots \bG_{K-1}\bP_K(x_{iK})\mathbf{1}.
\end{equation}
where $\bpi$ is the initial state probability vector, $\bP_t(x)$ is a diagonal matrix of observation likelihoods for each state ($[x|s],\ s=0,\dots,J+1$), $\bG_t$ is the probability transition matrix for states, and $\mathbf{1}$ is a vector of ones. Therefore, to evaluate the probability of a single capture-history, $\bx_i$, conditional on being captured at least once, we use a second iteration of the HMM formulation to obtain,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dJS}
 [\bx_i|\bt,c]  = \frac{[\bx_i|\bt]}{1-[\bx_i=\mathbf{0}|\bt]}
\end{equation}
where the denominator, $p^*=1-[\bx_i=\mathbf{0}|\bt]$, the probability of being captured at least once. Efficient HMM algorithms can be used to evaluate both the numerator and denominator because $1 - p^*$ is calculated by simply replacing the observed capture-history with all 0s and evaluating it with (\ref{eq.uncond.lik}) as well. The fully marginal posterior can be evaluated efficiently as,
\begin{equation}\label{eq.js.lik}
[\bt,\lambda|\bx] \propto \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{[\bx_i|\bt]}{1-[\bx_i=\mathbf{0}|\bt]}~ [n|\bt, \lambda][\bt][\lambda]
\end{equation}

For a traditional multistate JS model the matrices in the likelihood function (\ref{eq:dJS}) are given as follows. First, the initial state probability vector is 
%
\[
\bpi = \left[\begin{array}{cccccc} 
1-\tilde{\xi}_1 & \tilde{\xi}_1\alpha_{1,1} & \cdots & \tilde{\xi}_1\alpha_{1,J} & 0 \\
\end{array}\right],
\]
% 
 where $\tilde{\xi}_t = \xi_t /\sum_{j=t}^K \xi_j$, is the conditional probability that an individual enters between $t-1$ and $t$ given it has not entered prior to $t-1$. The probability transition matrix is, for $t=1,\dots,K-1$,
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq.gamma.mat}
\bG_t = 
\left[\begin{array}{ccccccc}
1-\tilde{\xi}_{t+1} & \tilde{\xi}_{t+1}\alpha_{1,t+1} & \cdots & \tilde{\xi}_{t+1}\alpha_{J,t+1} & 0\\
0 & \phi_{1,t} \psi_{1,1,t} & \cdots & \phi_{1,t} \psi_{1,J,t} & 1-\phi_{1,t}\\
\vdots & \vdots & & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & \phi_{J,t} \psi_{J,1,t} & \cdots & \phi_{J,t} \psi_{J,J,t} & 1-\phi_{J,t}\\
0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1
\end{array} \right]
\end{equation}

Now that we have an efficient JS likelihood formulation, the first stage MCMC can proceed using standard Metropolis-within-Gibbs updates for $\bt$ and $\lambda$. If only the demographic processes are of interest, one can just use (\ref{eq.js.lik}) to sample $\bt$. If the overall population abundance is desired, one can sample the number of uncaptured individuals, $N_u$, from the predictive distribution,
\[
[N_u|\bt, \lambda] = \text{Poisson}(\lambda (1-p^*)).
\] 
This results from the infinite augmentation scheme because 
\[
[N_u|\bt,\gamma] = \text{Binomial}(M, \gamma(1-p^*)) \to \text{Poisson}(N_u|\lambda(1-p^*))
\]
as $M\to\infty$. So, for each iteration of an MCMC algorithm it can be updated with a draw from a standard Poisson distribution as a posterior predictive variable. Then $N = N_u + n$ results directly \citep{johnson2010model, schofield2023estimating}. If other abundance quantities such as $N_t$ are desired, we must sample the individual states to calculate these within the second stage sampler. We can use Bayesian HMM algorithms to efficiently draw from those posterior predictive distributions as well. 


\subsection{Predicting states and abundance}

Now we turn our focus to drawing the second stage posterior predictive sample of states, $\bz^+$ and $\bw^+$, and associated abundance quantities. Because we are in an infinite augmentation paradigm, $\bz_{aug}$ and $\bw_{aug}$ are no longer well defined as they are infinite vectors. Therefore we switch to prediction of $\bz$ plus the states and time dependent abundance values of individuals that were never captured, $\bN_u = (N_{u,s,t})$ for $s=0,\dots,J+1$ and $t=1,\dots,K$. The second sample is drawn from the predictive distribution,
\begin{equation}
[\bz, \bN_u|N_u, \bt,\bx] = [\bz|\bt,\bx][\bN_u|N_u,\bt].
\end{equation} 

To sample the latent states of individuals in the population we first sample the states of the individuals captured at least once from $[\bz_i| \bt, \bx_i]$. This can be accomplished efficiently with the Forward--Filtering Backward--Sampling (FFBS) algorithm for conditional state sampling of HMMs \citep{scott2002bayesian}. The details of the of the FFBS algorithm can be found in the online supplement (Appendix A, algorithm 1). Heuristically, the algorithm works by first using the HMM forward algorithm \citep{zucchini2016hidden} followed by a backward pass through the capture history to sample from $[z_{it}|z_{i,t+1},\dots,z_{i,K},\bt, \bx_i]$ at each occasion. To facilitate this sampling algorithm we have coded the FFBS algorithm into the {\tt nimbleJSextras} package and it can be implemented as a stochastic node in model code statements. Once we perform the the FFBS algorithm for each captured individual we can calculate the derived quantity, $n_{s,t} = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathcal{I}(z_{it}=s)$, the abundance of individuals observed at least once in state $s$ at time $t$. For simplicity, we use the bold notation, $\bn_t = (n_{1,t},\dots,n_{J,t})$, to represent the vector of state-specific abundances and $n_t = \sum_{s=1}^{J} n_{s,t}$ to represent the total abundance of captured individuals available at time $t$. 

Now that we have the state and time-specific abundances for the observed individuals, we shift to the $N_u$ individuals that are never captured. We could use the same FFBS algorithm to individually sample the states of all $N_u$ individuals all with the capture-history $\bx_i=\mathbf{0}$. However, Because all uncaptured individuals have the same history, we can use a multinomial version of the FFBS, to sample the state and time-specific abundance in one forward and backward pass without simulating individual state histories (Online supplement Appendix A, algorithm 2). The {\tt nimbleJSextras} also contains a distribution for sampling the state and time specific counts of uncaptured individuals, $\mathbf{N}_u$. 
Now that we have posterior samples of $\bn_t$ and $\bN_{u}$ we can predict the state and time-specific abundances $\bN_t =  \bn_t + \bN_{u,t}$, and $N_t =  n_t + \sum_{s=1}^J N_{u,s,t}$, or any other function of state and time-specific abundance.   

\subsection{Additional computation benefits of the collapsed sampler} \label{sec.savings}

Here we demonstrate some additional computational savings that we can use due to the factoring of the posterior distribution in the collapsed Gibbs sampler. The first savings to note is that we only need to use the unique capture histories to evaluate the fully marginal likelihood \citep{turek2016efficient},
\begin{equation}
[\bx|\bt,\bc] = \prod_{i=1}^n [\bx_i|\bt,c] = \prod_{j=1}^{n^*} w_j[\bx_j^*|\bt,c]
\end{equation}
where $\bx_j^*$ is the $j$th unique capture-history, $w_j$ is the number of times it occurs in $\bx$ and $n^*$ is number of unique histories in $\bx$. This is standard practice when fitting models using maximum likelihood methods, however, it is not possible using the PX-DA approach for Bayesian analysis. This can be a substantial computational savings if $n^*$ is much smaller than $n$ \citep{turek2016efficient}. 

The second computational saving is due to the predictive factoring in the collapsed sampler. This is the same approach used by \cite{Hooten:2023aa}, and \cite{hooten2024geostatistical} to facilitate an efficient 2-stage approach to Bayesian estimation of closed-population models and we can extend this to open-population models. The 2-stage approach first involves drawing a posterior sample from $[\bt,\lambda|\bx]$, preferably using just the unique capture-histories. Then, divide the sample into $B$ batches and sample from $[\bz, \bN_u|N_u, \bt,\lambda,\bx]$ in parallel for each $(N_u, \bt,\lambda)$ in the separate batches. 


\section{Examples and Extensions}

\subsection{The Dipper Data}\label{sec:dipper}

The first examination of the ubiquitous European dipper data was presented by \cite{Cormack1964} who used it for demonstration of the Cormack-Jolly-Seber (CJS) model for survival estimation from capture-recapture data. Many years later \cite{Lebreton:1992gr} used the data to demonstrate a JS analysis for abundance estimation. More recently, \cite{Royle:2008kx} demonstrate a Bayesian version of JS modeling using PX-DA. Here we demonstrate analysis of the data using the {\it per capita} recruitment parameterization of \cite{link2005modeling} for modeling entry probability. This model is not available in Program MARK for the purposes of abundance estimation. So we used it here to demonstrate model development with \verb|nimbleJSextras|. 

\subsubsection{Model specifications}

The entry probabilities of the {\it per capita} recruitment model are given by 
\[
\xi_t \propto \left\{ \begin{array}{ll}
1  & t=1\\
d_t f_{t-1} & t=2,\dots,K
\end{array} \right.
\]
$d_t \propto E[N_t/N|\bt, N]$, the expected relative abundance, and $f_t$ is the {\it per capita} recruitment rate. The quantity $d_t$ is calculated with the recursion, $d_{t} = d_{t-1}(\phi_{t-1} + f_{t-1})$ where $d_1 \equiv 1$. 

In addition to the {\it per capita} recruitment parameterization of the entry model, we also use a hierarchical approach for estimation of the  parameters. The hierarchical model adds the random effects components,
\begin{center}
$[\text{logit } \phi_t] = N(\mu_\phi,\sigma_\phi^2)$ \\
$[\text{logit } p_t] = N(\mu_p,\sigma_p^2)$, and $[\text{log } f_t] = N(\mu_f,\sigma_f^2)$
\end{center}
This allows shrinkage of the full time model to the temporally constant model. We used the complexity prior distribution \citep{martins2017penalising} on each of the three the variance components, $\sigma_* \sim \text{Exponential}(1)$. All mean components of the random effect where given the vaguely informative $N(0,1.5^2)$ prior distribution. For $\lambda$ we used $[\lambda] =$ Gamma(1.0e-6, 1.0e-6) to approximate the objective prior, $[\lambda]=\lambda^{-1}$\citep{schofield2023estimating}.

Code for this example is provided in the online supplement and the dipper data was obtained from the {\tt marked} package \citep{Laake:2013aa}. We analyzed this same model with four different methods: (1) PX-DA with 300 augmented individuals, (2) PX-DA with 1,000 augmented individuals, (3) the collapsed sampler, and (4) the collapsed sampler using additional computational savings of Section (\ref{sec.savings}).  For the 2-stage collapsed sampler, we used 10 parallel sessions so that each session used 5,000 iterations from the first stage to sample abundance. We compared the first 5,000 iterations of methods to assess progress toward convergence using the scale reduction diagnostic \citep{GelmanRubin1992a} as well as the multivariate version \citep{BrooksGelman1998a}. For this diagnostic a value $\approx 1$ implies convergence. For full estimation we used 50,000 MCMC iterations following a 10,000 iteration burnin. 

\subsubsection{Results}

The multivariate scale reduction for the first 5,000 iterations of the collapsed approach was 1.06, while the scale reduction for the PX-DA approach with 300 and 1,000 augmented individuals was 1.43 and 1.25 respectively. So, we can see that convergence begins sooner for the collapsed sampler. It is unclear why the PX-DA (1,000) analysis seemed to converge at a faster rate. Perhaps the extra augmentation allowed the model to mix faster. 

To compare the relative run-times of each of the methods we hold PX-DA (300) as the default standard. The extra 700 augmented individuals in the second PX-DA analysis increased run-time by 3.63$\times$ PX-DA (300) analysis. Run-time for the standard collapsed sampler was 0.63$\times$ the PX-DA (300) analysis. Finally, the run-time of the two-stage parallel collapsed sampler was 0.11$\times$ the PX-DA (300) analysis, a substantial improvement over the PX-DA methods. For the dipper data there are only $n^* =32$ unique capture-histories in the $n=294$ observed individuals. So, the likelihood calculation only needs to loop over the 32 unique histories for each MCMC iteration. 

For each of the four methods, the parameter and abundance estimates aligned outside of Monte Carlo error, as expected. The super population size estimate is $N = 322.2$ with SD = 15.5 and 95\% highest probability density interval (HPD) of [299--353]. Yearly abundance estimates are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:dipper.Nt}. All other parameter estimates are presented in the online supplement (Table S1).

\subsection{Abundance and reproductive demographics of nesting Hawaiian green sea turtles}

Here we present an example analysis with a multistate component, abundance of female nesting Hawaiian green sea turtles ({\it Chelonia mydas}) or {\it honu}. This analysis demonstrates use of the 2-stage collapsed sampler to fit a model that incorporates individual by time effects on capture probability heterogeneity. Specifically we fitted a behavioral model with effect of previous capture on the current capture probability. In addition, these data are relatively large and PX-DA methods can be computationally expensive in that case. So, we can use the two-stage collapsed sampler to efficiently sample the posterior. 

The data presented here are from the 1973--2016 nesting season surveys on the islets within the atoll of Lalo (French Frigate Shoals), in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands. Over the 44 year study period $n$ = 5,271 females were captured. During the season, honu will migrate from the Main Hawaiian Islands (MHI) to nest at Lalo starting in late April through early August. After nesting is complete honu will return to the MHI and enter the foraging phase which lasts between 2--10 (average {\it c.} 4) years, where no nesting occurs \citep{BalazsEtAl2015a}. The time between nesting occasions is termed {\it remigration interval}. 


\subsubsection{Model Specifications}

There are two alterations to the traditional JS model that are necessary to estimate the size of the nesting female population with this data. The first issue is that individuals become unavailable for capture for an extended time while they are foraging because it is not feasible to capture individuals in the MHI with any systematic effort. During nesting phase individuals are concentrated at Lalo and relatively easy to capture. Second, processing individuals on their initial capture (i.e., unmarked animals) is more time consuming than recording individuals that already have a permanent identification (marked). In some years there is not enough total survey resources to equally process marked and unmarked individuals. Therefore, in some years capture and processing of unmarked individuals is lower priority than recording marked individuals. This is similar to a closed-population $M_b$ model \citep{OtisEtAl1978a} where the individuals become ``trap happy'', however, this mathematical behavioral response is due to capture effort on the part of the researchers, not actual honu behavior.

To model the effect of the remigration interval on abundance of hawksbill marine turtles ({\it Eretmochelys imbricata}) \citet{kendall2019multistate} used the breeding return time (JS-BRT) model of \cite{PledgerEtAl2013a} which is a special case of the semi-Markov capture-recpature model described by \cite{king2016semi}. The JS-BRT model uses several intermediate substates to represent the overall condition of the individual being in a nonbreeding state, e.g., for honu, the individual is in a foraging (nonbreeding) state if it is in any one of the substates, $F_1,\dots, \text{or } F_m$. The transition probabilities force the individual to pass through the substates sequentially or return to the nesting state. 
%With regard to the composite state, $F=\{F_1,\dots,F_m\}$, the underlying model is a Hidden {\it semi}-Markov model (HSMM), because the dwell-time in the composite state is not geometrically distributed as it would be in a strictly Markov chain.

Now we examine the effort-induced trap response issue. In a PX-DA analysis this would be taken care of with a individual $\times$ occasion covariate, say $c_{it}$ that indicates if individual $i$ has been previously captured on occasion $t$. We cannot use that approach in the collapsed sampler because we are not explicitly simulating state-histories for uncaptured individuals. However, we can use the another set of substates to define our reproductive state: $R_{00}$, $R_{01}$, $R_{10}$, $R_{11}$, where the subscripts indicate, respectively, that an individual has been previously captured and is currently captured on occasion $t$. We have moved the capture process into the state transitions. In addition to the composite reproductive state, we also need to further expand the foraging substates to $F_{01},\dots,F_{0m},F_{11},\dots,F_{1m}$ where the first subscript denotes previous capture. These additional foraging states are not biologically different, they simply serve to prevent transitions among the reproductive states that are not permitted, e.g., if an individual is in $R_{00}$ its next reproductive state cannot be $R_{11}$ or $R_{10}$.

The model specifications for this model are notably different from the standard JS form described previously. However, It can still be placed in the same HMM framework for which we can use the collapsed Gibbs sampler with the additional computational savings benefits. The HMM matrix specifications are as follows.

We begin with the state transition matrix which can be represented in its general form with the block structure,
\[
\bG_t = \left[\begin{array}{cccc}
1-\tilde{\xi}_{t+1} & \tilde{\xi}_{t+1}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\emptyset,R,t} & \mathbf{0} & 0\\
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & \phi_{R,t}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{R,F,t}  & (1-\phi_{R,t})\mathbf{1} \\
\mathbf{0} & \phi_{F,t}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{F,R,t} &  \phi_{F,t}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{F,F,t} & (1-\phi_{F,t})\mathbf{1} \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{array}
\right],
\] 
where the $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{a,b,t}$ matrices represent the submatrix describing transitions from the composite state ``$a$'' to composite state ``$b$'' \citep{langrock2011hidden,king2016semi}. The $1\times 4$ entry submatrix is, $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\emptyset,R,t} = [1-\eta_{t+1} p_{t+1}\ \ \eta_{t+1} p_{t+1}\ \ 0\ \ 0]$, where $p_t$ is the capture-probability and $0 \le \eta_t \le 1$ is the proportional reduction in capture probability due to reduced effort for capturing unmarked individuals in year $t$. The submatrix $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{R,F,t}$ is a $4 \times 2m$ indicator matrix such that $R_{00}$ transitions to $F_{01}$ and $R_{01}$, $R_{10}$, and $R_{11}$ transition to $F_{11}$ automatically because honu cannot nest in subsequent years. The $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{F,R,t}$, matrix is composed of elements,
\[
\psi_{F_{0j},R_{00},t} = \tau_{j}(1-\eta_{t+1}p_{t+1}),\qquad \psi_{F_{0j},R_{01},t} = \tau_j\eta_{t+1} p_{t+1} 
\]
\[
\psi_{F_{1j},R_{10},t} = \tau_j(1-p_{t+1}),\qquad \psi_{F_{1j},R_{11},t} = \tau_j p_{t+1} 
\]
for $j=1,\dots,m$ where $\tau_j$ is the probability of transitioning from foraging substate $j$ to a reproductive state. All other $F$ to $R$ transitions have probability zero. Finally, the $\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{F,F,t}$ matrix is composed of elements,
\[
\psi_{F_{0j},F_{0,j+1},t} = 1-\tau_j \text{ for } j=1,\dots,m-1, \text{ and } \psi_{F_{0m},F_{0m},t} = 1-\tau_{m}.
\]
The $F_1$ transitions are identical and all other probabilities are 0.

The bulk of the model specification falls in the transition matrix. The other HMM matrices are, comparatively, more straightforward. The initial entry vector is,
\[
\bpi = \left[1-\tilde{\xi}_1\quad \tilde{\xi}_1(1-\eta_1 p_1) \quad \tilde{\xi}_1\eta_1 p_1\quad \mathbf{0}\right]
\]
where $\mathbf{0}$ is a $2m+3$ row vector of zeros. The observation matrices, $\bP(x_{i,t})$, are still diagonal with $[x_{i,t}|s]$ entries. However, because the detection process has been absorbed in the state transition, $[x_{i,t}|s]$ is just an indicator that the observation $x_{i,t}$ is possible in state $s$. For example $[x_{i,t}=0|s=R_{00}]=1$ and $[x_{i,t}=1|s=R_{00}]=0$. 

Now that we have the general form of the HMM matrices, we can define specific parameterization for this analysis. Honu are long-lived with very stable survival once they reach adulthood \citep{PiacenzaEtAl2016a} so we modeled survival probability as constant over time and between states, $\phi_{s,t} = \phi$ with prior distribution $[\text{logit }\phi] = t(\sigma=1.52,\text{df}=6)$, where $t(\sigma,\text{df})$ is a scaled $t$ distribution with df degrees of freedom. We used this specification to closely approximate a uniform distribution for $\phi$ but the sampled parameter, $\text{logit }\phi$, is unconstrained. 

Because we modeling occasion specific capture probabilities for unmarked individuals, allowing unrestricted entry probabilities (outside of sum-to-one constraint) is somewhat confounded in the model. Because entry to the state of reproductive adult is the culmination of $\sim$20 years of survival and growth from hatching \citep{HoutanEtAl2014a} we assume that this process is relatively smooth from year to year. To model this autocorrelation we used the basis function $\text{mlogit }\boldsymbol{\xi}= \mathbf{B}\bb$, where the coefficients have distribution $[\beta_k|\sigma_{\xi}] = N(0,\sigma_{\xi}^2)$ \citep{hefley2016basis}. To avoid over smoothing we used a Mat\'{e}rn ($\nu=1.5$; \citealt{kammann2003geoadditive}) function as a basis instead of a Gaussian radial basis function. We used 10 evenly spaced internal knots and 4 external knots on each end outside of [1--$K$] to eliminate boundary effects for a total of 18 knots. To complete the specification we used the complexity penalty prior distribution $[\sigma_{\xi}] = \text{exponential}(1)$ \citep{martins2017penalising} to shrink $\xi_t$ to uniform $1/K$ if the data do not support an occasion specific entry process.  

%using recruitment probability \citep{Royle:2008kx}. The recruitment probability, $\rho_t$, is the probability that the individual enters at occasion $t$ given it has not yet entered. This essentially mirrors the exit probability. To use it in the collapsed sampler we need the unconditional entry probability,
%\[
%\xi_t \propto \left\{
%\begin{array}{ll}
%\rho_1 & t=1 \\
%\rho_t \prod_{j=1}^{t-1} (1-\rho_j) & t \ge 2
%\end{array}, \right.
%\]
%from which we obtain $\tilde{\xi}_t$ for the transition matrices. We, again, used the logit transformation to specify the prior distributions $[\text{logit }\rho_t] = t(1.52,6)$. 

For the capture probability parameters, $p_t$ and $\eta_t$ we used the $t(1.52,6)$ prior distributions as well as setting $\eta_1=\eta_2 \equiv 1$ because all individuals are unmarked on occasions 1 and 2. Finally, we used $m=5$ foraging substates to model the remigration interval, with $[\text{logit }\tau_j] = t(1.52,6)$. This choice of $m$ results in $J=4+2(5)=14$ active states and 16 total states for the model. Finally, we used the approximate objective prior $[\lambda]=$ Gamma(1.0e-6, 1.0e-6).

Using the two-stage approach we ran the MCMC sampler for 250,000 iterations following a 50,000 iteration burn-in, only every fifth iteration was retained for storage. Out of 5,271 observed individuals, there were only 1,338 unique capture histories, so, the sample size burden in the first stage is reduced by about 75\%. Anecdotally, as all computation environments are different, on an Apple MacBook Pro with an M2 Max processor, the first-stage MCMC took approximately 3.5 hours to run. For the second stage we used 10 parallel sessions and this completed in approximately 7.7 minutes.

\section{Discussion}

Here we presented motivation and examples demonstrating that Bayesian inference for estimating abundance from capture-recapture data using Jolly-Seber family models does not necessarily need the PX-DA approach to be practical for regular use. By collapsing the PX-DA Gibbs sampler we can eliminate all need for state and data augmentation. Although there are no MCMC theories we can directly use to guarantee convergence improvements due to the Metropolis-within-Gibbs samplers that are used in practice. It is often noted that convergence improvements are realized in practice \citep{VanDykJiao2015a}. In fact, in the dipper example we did see some initial convergence improvement, although only slight. However, more importantly, the collapsed sampler allows us to use some additional computational tricks which can drastically speed up the MCMC, only using unique capture histories and parallel sampling of any desired abundance quantities. 

To make the collapsed approach straightforward for practitioners to use we developed the {\tt R} package \verb|nimbleJSextras|, which can be installed from Github or R-universe \footnote{\url{https://dsjohnson.r-universe.dev/nimbleJSextras}}. Both source and binary installations are available from R-universe. The package extends the \verb|nimble| and \verb|nimbleEcology| packages for fitting JS models using the collapsed approach. With \verb|nimbleJSextras| fitting a JS models requires relatively the same level of bespoke  coding as the PX-DA approach. In addition to the multistate models shown in the example section we have also included functions for fitting Binomial and Poisson capture histories as well. The code in these extra versions serves as a template for those that might want to extend to other types of capture-histories. 

The examples of Section 4 illustrate both a standard JS analysis with the dipper data, as well as, inclusion of individual $\times$ occasion effects on capture heterogeneity in the honu analysis. But it is useful to consider when the PX-DA approach might be preferred. One of the main benefits of the PX-DA approach over even standard frequentist methods such as maximum likelihood estimation is the ease of which all types of heterogeneity can be included in a model. For example, individual heterogeneity in survival or capture probability can easily be included with an individual random effect within logit specifications of the parameters (see \citealt{Royle:2008kx}, Chapter 10). These continuously valued random effects would be difficult if not prohibitively challenging to implement with the collapsed approach. However, we speculate that, as in the honu example, there may be finite state alternatives or approximations for these models which could produce similar results. \citet{Pledger:2010fk} note that finite state models with 2 states can be effective approximations for continuous capture heterogeneity models. However, a model with many different continuous random effects components might greatly expand the number of states necessary to approximate said model. So, while a single random effect for individual capture heterogeneity might be easily approximated by a few states, the number of states needed for heterogeneity in capture, survival, and state transitions will grow multiplicatively. In that case, PX-DA models may be easier to implement, but, some further investigation is warranted. For example, \citet{johnson2016multivariate}, show that multivariate states can be represented more parsimoniously than a full product of levels. 

Astute readers will notice, while we used the collapsed Gibbs sampler to derive a fully marginal approach, in the end we arrived at a standard Bayesian analysis in which we use the exact data likelihood and prior distributions to arrive at posterior inference. For the true model parameters: $\bp$, $\bphi$, $\bxi$, and $\lambda$, this is, indeed, the case. However, using the derivation of the collapsed sampler to arrive at this realization serves three purposes. First, it allows us to use some theoretical justification to provide some assurance that there is a very high likelihood that we will improve the MCMC mixing and convergence rate versus the PX-DA version. \cite{yackulic2020need} also demonstrated this for marginalized Cormack-Jolly-Seber (CJS) models through simulation. Although, we are not mathematically guaranteed that this will occur. Second, the collapsed Gibbs sampler allows us to explicitly formulate abundance values, e.g., $N_1,\dots,N_K$, as posterior predictive quantities, which is not immediately obvious from classical likelihood derivations such as \cite{schwarz1996general}. This allows the use of the parallel sampling in the second stage. Finally, the process of deriving the collapsed Gibbs sampler allows us to connect the traditional frequentist JS likelihood to the the Bayesian PX-DA version, which for many practitioners has become the default way to think about abundance estimation from capture-recapture data. 

Given that we are using the exact likelihood in a traditional Bayesian analysis we can also examine some implications of our development in that light. The first thing to note is that $N$ is not a parameter in our likelihood, $\lambda$ takes its place. In the \cite{schwarz1996general} JS likelihood, $[n|\bt, N] = \text{Binomial}(N,p^*)$ is used instead of $\text{Poisson}(\lambda p^*)$ \citep{king2015capture}. In a traditional Bayesian analysis using the likelihood we would put a prior distribution on $N$. If we assume $[N] = \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$ we will arrive at the same likelihood here, developed from allowing $M \to \infty$, after marginalizing over $N$. However, \cite{link2013cautionary} suggests the objective prior $[N]=N^{-1}$. But as \cite{schofield2023estimating} proves, using the Poisson prior with hyper-prior $[\lambda]=\lambda^{-1}$ will give the exact same posterior inference as when using the objective prior for $N$ directly. So, while it may seem that we are making a specific distributional assumption for $N$ by using the Poisson distribution, it is not as consequential as it might seem. In the example sections we used $[\lambda] = \text{Gamma}(\epsilon, \epsilon)$ which approximates $\lambda^{-1}$ for $\epsilon \to 0$. So, the collapsed development provides a framework to complete the arc of Bayesian inference for JS models from the exact likelihood of \cite{schwarz1996general} to the PX-DA expansion of \cite{Durban:2005yw} and \cite{Royle:2007kx} and back again to use of the exact likelihood.
